You walk away from the crash feeling fine. A day or two later your neck
stiffens, a headache sets in, and turning your head to check your blind spot
hurts. This is the classic story of whiplash — and the delayed start is one of
the things that makes it so easy to ignore until it gets worse.

## What whiplash is

In a collision — even a low-speed one — your head can snap forward and back
faster than your neck muscles can control. That sudden motion strains the
muscles and ligaments of the neck and stresses the small joints between the
vertebrae. The result is the cluster of symptoms people call whiplash: neck
pain and stiffness, headaches (often starting at the base of the skull),
reduced range of motion, and sometimes pain or tingling into the shoulders and
arms.

## Why symptoms are often delayed

Right after an accident, adrenaline and shock can mask injury. Inflammation
builds over the following hours and days, and that is when the pain typically
arrives. Feeling fine at the scene and worse on day three is completely normal
— it does not mean the injury is minor, and it does not mean you are making it
up. It is just how soft-tissue inflammation works.

## Why early treatment matters

The old advice was to rest in a stiff collar and wait. In most cases, that
turns out to be exactly the wrong approach. Guided, early movement tends to
lead to better recovery than prolonged immobilization, because stiffness and a
fear of moving tend to settle in when the neck is left still for too long.

In physical therapy we work to restore your range of motion, retrain the deep
neck muscles that stabilize your head, and ease the tension headaches that
usually ride along with whiplash. Recovery time varies — we set your milestones
at your first visit rather than promising a fixed schedule — but starting
sooner generally means fewer compensations to undo later.
