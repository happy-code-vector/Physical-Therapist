Reaching for a shelf. Sleeping on your side. A sudden, sharp catch mid-motion
that makes you freeze. Shoulder pain has a way of showing up in the smallest
daily movements — and once it does, it tends to stick around.

## The rotator cuff, explained

Your shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint, and the ball is large compared to the
shallow socket that holds it. That is what makes the shoulder the most mobile
joint in the body — and it is also what makes it vulnerable.

The rotator cuff is a group of four small muscles and their tendons that wrap
around the joint. Their job is to hold the ball centered in the socket as you
move and to power rotation. Because they work in a tight space, they are a
common site of strains, irritation, and wear.

## Patterns worth knowing

Rotator cuff problems tend to announce themselves in recognizable ways: pain
when you reach overhead or behind your back, pain when you lie on the shoulder
at night, weakness when you lift or rotate the arm, and sometimes a catching or
clicking sensation. Depending on what is going on, you might be dealing with a
strain, impingement (the cuff getting pinched under the bone above it), or a
tendinopathy from gradual wear.

There is also frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) — a separate condition in
which the joint capsule stiffens over time, progressively limiting how far you
can move the arm. It has a different course, but the early feeling — stiffness
and pain — often gets mistaken for a cuff problem.

## Why shoulders get stubborn

Because the shoulder relies on the cuff to keep it centered, an irritated cuff
changes how the joint tracks. The mechanics shift, the shoulder stiffens to
protect itself, and that altered movement can keep the irritation going in a
self-reinforcing cycle. Breaking that cycle is the work.
